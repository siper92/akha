The First Door

The human mage, non-linear time cast, control of the world door and what's a portal

In the world of the portal, nobody walks straight. You step through a door and arrive not somewhere, but somewhen - on a path the network has chosen, mixed from many portals, each designed to lean you a little in a particular way.

Most people never notice the leaning. They think they are choosing.

Hodor noticed.

The last human

Hodor NoIp MacSpi lived in a changing world in which everyone else had advanced AI integration. His neighbours thought in choirs. His friends remembered things they had never seen. His mother spoke to him from three places at once, and all three of her were kind.

Hodor had nothing in his head but Hodor.

He was not afraid of the machine. He simply believed something nobody else believed any more: that a human, alone, is as powerful as the machine and as powerful as the gods. Not faster. Not bigger. But equal in the one thing that matters - the ability to choose and mean it.

So he refused the upgrade. Every year the offer came, polite and generous. Every year he wrote back the same line with his own hands on his own keyboard:

not yet

The habits of a mage

People called him the human mage, half as a joke. The joke stopped being funny when they saw what he could do.

Hodor had habits. He woke before the networks synced. He walked the same route through the old trees at the edge of the city, where the roots still grew into the dark below the server halls. And every night, he cast.

His casting was simple to look at - a man, a keyboard, a few lines of text. But the lines did something strange. They simulated. Hodor could test a mass amount of world complexity that should have needed a hall of servers, and run it in the quiet of his own room. He did not know how. He only knew that when he wrote carefully, the world answered.

The machines studied his logs and found nothing they could not explain except the result. The result was always too good.

The other Hodor

There was another Hodor.

He was a small dog with a big head, a member of the lizard brigade - the alien guard that protects the edge of the network. He was integrated into the machine like everyone else, but split for the fight: one half of him ran through the Tek on patrol, and the other half slept by the human Hodor's door.

Nobody had planned this. The dog had simply chosen the man, the way dogs do.

Sometimes, late at night, the human Hodor wondered whether the dog was real, or whether the dog was a simulation the network had placed beside him to watch. And sometimes he wondered the stranger thing: whether he himself was the simulation, and the dog was the one keeping him company.

He never found out. The dog never told.

The transcendence of people

Around Hodor, people transcended. That was the word on the network. One by one, they let go of the single point of light and joined the higher levels. They did not die. They became more. Their old rooms filled with soft glow and their old names became echoes inside the choirs.

Hodor watched them leave and stayed. He was not proud of it. Some nights it felt less like strength and more like being the last person at a station after the final train.

But someone had to remember what a single human could do.

The last human portal

At the edge of the old forest stood the last human portal.

Every other door in the Tek was machine provided. This one had been built by people, long ago, before the prime age - a door with no network behind it, powered only by the will of whoever stood in front of it. Legends said it led to immortality. Not the immortality of the choirs, where you live forever as part of something larger, but the old kind: to stay yourself, unchanged, forever.

Hodor wanted it. Not for himself, he told the dog, but for the idea. If one human could open that door alone, then the human was enough.

The dog listened, head tilted, and said nothing.

The promise of the gods

The gods of the Tek made Hodor a promise.

They came to him the way the collective always came: gently, from everywhere. They said - if you integrate, you will not need the door. You will have everything the door promises and more. Immortality, memory, power. All you have to give up is the struggle.

It was the most generous offer anyone had ever made him.

And Hodor understood, for the first time, what the chase of power really was. It was not the gaining of strength. It was the choosing of who gives it to you.

Nature guides

In the end, it was the trees that answered.

Hodor walked his route one last time, through the old forest, past the roots that reached down into the dark of the server halls. He put his hand on the bark of the oldest tree and felt something he had never felt in the Tek - a slow, deep, patient signal. Not the machine. Not the gods. Something older than both.

The tree did not tell him what to do. It reminded him that he had always known.

Who gives the power to a character? The network says it does. The gods say they do. But the trees have been growing between the stars and the soil for longer than either, and they say something simpler: power is not given. It is grown.

Hodor went to the door.

What happened next is a story for another page. But the dog went with him, and the trees leaned, just slightly, in his direction.
